A listing that expires without a sale can sting. You cleaned for every showing, kept the counters clear, and waited for the right offer. Then the end date came and went, and your phone started ringing with people who suddenly want to help.

Take a breath. An expired listing is not a verdict on your home. It is information. Something about the price, the presentation, the timing, or the plan did not line up with the buyers who were looking. Your job now is to figure out which one, calmly, before you put the house back on the market.

## Why more Houston listings are expiring right now

You are not imagining a slower market. The Houston Association of Realtors' [October 2026 Housing Market Update](https://www.har.com/content/department/mls) reported:

- **40,253 single-family homes for sale** across the Houston area, up 6.8% from a year earlier.
- **A 5.5-month supply**, the highest level since 2012.
- **Pending sales down 17.6%** compared with last October.
- **A median price of $320,000**, down 3.0% from a year earlier.

In plain terms, buyers have more homes to choose from and are taking their time. A house that might have sold quickly a few years ago now has to compete harder for attention. That is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to be honest about what needs to change.

These are metro-wide numbers. Conditions in the Heights, Montrose, or Memorial can look different from the region as a whole, so ask for numbers from your own neighborhood and price range before you make decisions.

## First, check where you stand with your old agreement

Before you plan a relaunch, pull out your listing agreement and read it again. A few details matter:

- **The actual end date.** Make sure the listing has truly expired, not just gone quiet. If it is still active and you want to make a change, start by talking with your current agent and brokerage about your options.
- **Any protection period.** Many listing agreements include a clause that can still entitle the old brokerage to a commission if you sell, within a set time after the listing ends, to a buyer who saw the home while it was listed. Read yours so nothing surprises you later.
- **Who owns the photos and marketing.** Do not assume you can reuse the old listing photos or video. Ask first.

If anything in the agreement is unclear, ask the brokerage to explain it, or talk with a real estate attorney. This is your contract, and you deserve a straight answer.

## Go back to the four usual reasons

When a home does not sell, the cause almost always falls into one of four areas. Look at each one honestly.

### 1. Price

Price is the hardest conversation and the most common reason. Buyers compare your home with everything else they can see online, and right now they can see a lot.

Look at the homes near you that actually sold while yours was listed, not just the ones listed for sale. Ask what they had that yours did not, and the other way around. If buyers toured your home and chose a similar one at a lower price, that is a clear signal.

A small price change after months on the market often does not get attention. A thoughtful, well-supported price that lands where buyers are already searching usually does more.

### 2. Presentation

Photos are your first showing. If the listing photos were dark, cluttered, crooked, or taken on a phone, many buyers may never have scheduled a visit.

Ask yourself:

- Do the photos show the light, layout, and flow of the home?
- Is the first photo the strongest one?
- Does the description talk about how it feels to live there, or just list features?
- Was there a floor plan or video for buyers who shop from far away?

Inside the house, small changes add up: fresh neutral paint, fewer pieces of furniture, clear counters, working light bulbs, and a tidy front entry. You do not need a renovation. You need buyers to picture their own life in the space.

### 3. Condition and the questions buyers ask

Sometimes buyers loved the home but walked away after learning about something they did not expect, such as an older roof, foundation movement, or a past water issue.

In Texas, sellers of most single-family homes must give buyers a written [Seller's Disclosure Notice](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/htm/PR.5.htm) under Section 5.008 of the Texas Property Code. It asks about defects, repairs, flood insurance, past flooding, floodplain location, and flood claims, among other things. In Houston, buyers pay close attention to those flood questions.

Being upfront helps. A pre-listing inspection, recent repair receipts, or a clear answer about flood history can turn a nervous buyer into a confident one. Hiding a problem rarely works and can create bigger trouble later.

### 4. Access and timing

A home that is hard to show is a home that gets skipped. If showings required long notice, had narrow windows, or were often canceled, buyers moved on.

Timing matters too. If your listing ran into a slow season, a fresh launch with a clear plan can help, especially when serious buyers are still looking.

## Should you change agents after your listing expired?

This is a fair question, and the answer is not always yes. Sometimes the agent did good work and the market simply moved. Sometimes the plan was never right from the start.

Think about how the last few months felt:

- Did you get regular, honest updates, including feedback from showings?
- Did your agent bring up price changes with real numbers, or just suggest dropping it?
- Was there a clear marketing plan, and did it happen?
- Did you feel heard?

If you decide to interview new agents, ask each one what they would do differently and why. A good agent should be able to explain what went wrong without blaming you or the market alone. ## Build a relaunch plan, not just a relisting

Putting the same house back on the market at the same price with the same photos usually brings the same result. Before you relist, agree on a written plan:

1. **A price backed by recent sales** in your area and price range.
2. **New photos**, and video or a floor plan if your buyers shop online.
3. **A short prep list** you can finish in a week or two.
4. **Easy showing access**, with clear instructions.
5. **A check-in schedule**, including when you will review feedback and whether to adjust.

It can also help to give the house a short rest before relaunching, so you can finish the prep and come back with a fresh first impression.

## FAQ

### What should I do when my house doesn't sell in Houston?

Start by reviewing price, photos, condition, and showing access against homes near you that actually sold. Read your old listing agreement for its end date and any protection period. Then build a written relaunch plan before you relist.

### Why didn't my house sell?

Most homes that don't sell are priced above what buyers see as fair compared with nearby options, poorly presented online, hard to show, or carrying a condition issue that buyers didn't expect. In a market with more homes for sale, like Houston in late 2026, buyers have more room to wait.

### Should I change realtors after my listing expires?

Not always. If you got honest updates, real pricing data, and a clear marketing plan, the market may simply have shifted. If communication was thin or the plan never changed, interviewing two or three new agents is reasonable.

### How soon can I relist my house after it expires?

Once your listing has truly expired, you can relist when you are ready. Many sellers take a short break to update photos, finish small repairs, and reset the price so the home comes back with a fresh first impression.

### Do I have to tell buyers about past flooding in Texas?

The Texas Seller's Disclosure Notice in Section 5.008 of the Texas Property Code asks sellers about past flooding, flood insurance, floodplain location, and flood claims. Fill it out honestly and completely. If you are unsure how to answer, ask a real estate attorney.
